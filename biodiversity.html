Biodiversity of Cascadia

The Cascadia bioregion is one of the most ecologically diverse temperate regions on Earth — temperate rainforests, alpine meadows, marine estuaries, and sagebrush steppes within a day's drive. This page aggregates citizen-science observations from iNaturalist (CC-BY licensed) to visualize what's living here, where, and when.

Field Research Travel Guide
When to go, where to look, and what to bring for wildlife researchers, photographers, and naturalists visiting Cascadia. iNaturalist + USFWS + Parks Canada
🌿 Spring (March — May)
Wildflower Blooms & Bird Migration

What to see: Pacific rhododendron blooms (April-May, Olympic NP), camas lily meadows (May, Willamette Valley), rufous hummingbird arrival (March), gray whale northbound migration (March-May, Depoe Bay OR).

Best locations: Olympic National Park (wildflowers), Ridgefield NWR (waterfowl), Camassia Natural Area (OR), Depoe Bay (whales).
Weather: Cool & wet. Bring rain gear, waterproof boots. Trails muddy.
Permits: Northwest Forest Pass for trailheads; WA Discover Pass for state parks.

☀️ Summer (June — August)
Alpine Wildlife & Marine Life

What to see: Mountain goats (Mt Rainier, Cascade crest), marmots (subalpine meadows July-Aug), sea otters (Vancouver Island, Olympic Coast), tufted puffins (Tatoosh Island, Haystack Rock), orcas (San Juan Islands July-Aug).

Best locations: Hurricane Ridge (marmots), San Juan Islands (orcas), Pacific Rim NP (sea otters), Mt Hood (alpine wildflowers).
Weather: Best weather but crowded. Book campsites 6+ months ahead. Wildfire smoke possible Aug-Sept.
Permits: Wilderness permits for overnight backcountry; ferry reservations for San Juans.

🍂 Fall (September — November)
Salmon Runs & Elk Rut

What to see: Salmon spawning runs (Sept-Nov, peak Oct), elk rut bugling (Sept-Oct, Olympic NP), raptor migration (Sep-Oct, Bonney Butte OR), snow geese arrival (Nov, Skagit Valley).

Best locations: Cedar River (Sept salmon), Issaquah Salmon Hatchery (Oct), Adams River BC (sockeye run, next peak 2026), Hoh Rainforest (elk rut), Fir Island (snow geese).
Weather: Increasingly wet. Wildfire smoke possible early Sept. Trail closures during heavy rain.
Highlight: Adams River Sockeye Run (every 4 years, next big run 2026) — 4+ million salmon.

❄️ Winter (December — February)
Eagle Congregations & Wintering Birds

What to see: Bald eagle congregations (Nov-Feb, Skagit River — 500+ birds), trumpeter swans (Padilla Bay), snow geese (Skagit Valley, 50,000+), gray whale southbound (Dec-Feb), owls (barred, great horned).

Best locations: Skagit River (eagle floats), Fir Island (snow geese), Ridgefield NWR (waterfowl), Reifel Migratory Bird Sanctuary BC.
Weather: Cold, wet, short days. Bring layers, waterproof gear. Some high-elevation trails closed due to snow.
Tip: Eagle watching best 8-10am on cold clear mornings after salmon carcasses wash downstream.

Research Resources & Citizen Science
Tools and platforms for researchers, students, and amateur naturalists studying Cascadia biodiversity.
Observation Platforms

iNaturalist — 313k+ Cascadia observations. Free app, AI species ID, research-grade data flows to GBIF.
eBird — Cornell Lab. Bird checklists, real-time sightings. Free, requires account.
e-Butterfly — Butterfly tracking, similar to eBird.
HerpMapper — Amphibian & reptile records.
Bumble Bee Atlas — Xerces Society, PNW surveys.

Museum Collections

Burke Museum (UW Seattle) — 16M specimens, ornithology + mammalogy + herbarium.
UBC Beaty Biodiversity Museum — 2M+ specimens, Vancouver BC.
OSU Herbarium — 500k plant specimens, Corvallis OR.
Royal BC Museum — Victoria, natural history collections.
Smithsonian NMNH — National collections, online portal.

Field Research Permits

National Parks — Research Permit Application System (irma.nps.gov). Free; 6-8 week review.
USFWS Refuges — Special Use Permits via local refuge office.
WA DNR — Research Permit for state trust lands.
BC Parks — Research Permit via BC Parks (env.gov.bc.ca).
Parks Canada — Research & Collection Permit (pc.gc.ca).

Conservation Projects — Next Decade (2025-2035)
Major biodiversity, salmon restoration, and Indigenous-led conservation projects across Cascadia. All budgets from public sources. Includes government + Indigenous-led initiatives. PUBLIC + INDIGENOUS-LED
🦅 Conservation Decade Outlook

Cascadia's biodiversity faces simultaneous climate stress and restoration opportunity over the next decade. Key trends:

  • Salmon recovery at a crossroads: $127M+ annual investment across Columbia River + Puget Sound, but 2025-26 Snake River dam decision could unlock $1.5B+ additional restoration. Tribal treaty rights litigation accelerating the timeline.
  • Indigenous-led conservation scaling: Canada's $340M CAD + $173M Guardians = largest Indigenous conservation investment in Canadian history. WA/OR tribal programs expanding but underfunded relative to need.
  • Wildfire resilience meets biodiversity: $700M+ dedicated to wildfire resilience, but forest treatments must balance fire risk reduction with habitat protection. Indigenous forestry knowledge critical.
  • Cross-border species recovery: Southern Resident orcas (73 individuals), Columbia salmon, marbled murrelet all require coordinated US-CA action. No formal bioregional conservation treaty exists.
  • Climate-driven range shifts: Subalpine species losing habitat; sagebrush species expanding northward; marine species shifting northward ~30km/decade. Conservation planning must account for dynamic ranges.

All budgets from public sources: USFWS, WA RCO, WA Legislature, BC Budget 2026, Environment Canada, Indigenous Guardians Program.

About the Data
iNaturalist is a citizen-science platform where naturalists upload photos of organisms they observe. Observations are licensed by their photographers under Creative Commons (mostly CC-BY, CC-BY-NC, or CC-BY-SA). The iNaturalist API is free, requires no API key, and returns JSON.
Quality grade filters observations to "research" grade — meaning the community has agreed on the identification. This ensures the species labels are reliable. Casual observations are excluded from this gallery.
Taxonomic scope covers all life — plants, animals, fungi, protists, bacteria. The Cascadia bioregion has approximately 50,000+ cataloged species across its 5 ecoregions (Marine West Coast Forest, Marine West Coast Alpine, Cascadia Mixed Forest, Eastern Cascadia Slopes, Columbia Plateau).
