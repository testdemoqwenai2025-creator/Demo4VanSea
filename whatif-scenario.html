Model Methodology

Understanding the linear projection models behind the scenario engine

Fiscal Models

Revenue impacts are modeled using population-weighted baseline figures. BC revenue uses 5.1M population multiplied by 12 months for MSP premium calculations, yielding approximately $4.6B in annual MSP revenue at the default $75/month rate. The corporate tax base of approximately $70B in taxable corporate income generates ~$18.9B at the 27% rate. Carbon tax revenue uses the Province of British Columbia's published carbon tax yield data, averaging ~$45M per $1/tonne increment across all taxed emissions sources including transportation, buildings, and industry. WA revenue modeling uses the state's $1.8T gross state product for B&O tax calculations, a $400B retail sales base for sales tax projections, and an estimated $30B annual realized capital gains base derived from IRS and WA DOR data.

Employment Elasticity

Job creation/loss estimates use sectoral employment multipliers derived from BC Stats and WA ESD historical data. Corporate tax changes yield approximately 8,500 jobs per percentage point in BC, reflecting the province's 2.6M-person labor force and the concentration of employment in tax-sensitive sectors like technology, natural resources, and professional services. B&O tax changes yield approximately 45,000 jobs per percentage point in WA due to the broad base of the gross receipts tax and its outsized impact on small and medium enterprises. Minimum wage effects model both the positive income effect (increased consumer spending power driving service sector demand) and negative employment displacement effect (reduced hiring at the margin by labor-intensive businesses), with a net elasticity calibrated to Pacific Northwest regional studies.

Cross-Border Dynamics

Talent flow and business relocation models compare relative tax competitiveness between BC and WA using a composite tax burden index. The model incorporates corporate income tax, personal income tax (BC only), sales/excise taxes, property tax differentials, and payroll tax differentials. When one jurisdiction becomes significantly more favorable on the composite index, a proportional talent and business migration is projected based on historical Cascadia corridor flow patterns observed between 2015-2024. The model accounts for the friction of cross-border relocations including regulatory compliance costs, immigration processing, and cultural/quality-of-life factors that create natural stickiness. Trade volume projections incorporate the direct effects of tax changes on export competitiveness and import demand, as well as secondary effects through GDP and exchange rate channels.

Environmental Projections

Carbon emission changes are derived from the empirically observed carbon tax price elasticity of demand of approximately 0.18% reduction per $10/tonne increase, based on BC's own experience since implementing carbon pricing in 2008—the longest-running carbon tax in North America. This elasticity captures fuel switching, efficiency improvements, and behavioral changes across transportation, building heating, and industrial processes. Renewable energy adoption projections use the historical deployment response to policy signals in BC and WA, where renewable portfolio standards, clean fuel standards, and electrification mandates have driven steady adoption rates of 1-2% annually per major policy intervention. Clean tech investment projections are modeled based on venture capital and corporate R&D spending patterns in the Pacific Northwest, where climate-tech and clean-energy startups have attracted increasing shares of regional VC funding.

Housing Market Model

Housing price impacts model supply-side effects of the incentive fund at approximately 15,000 additional units per $1B in provincial investment, calibrated against BC Housing's annual construction completion data and CMHC housing supply reports. The price elasticity of approximately 2.8% per $1B in new supply is derived from the observed relationship between housing starts and price movements in Metro Vancouver and the Fraser Valley between 2010-2024, controlling for interest rate effects and population growth. WA housing effects capture minimum wage and tax impacts on household purchasing power and demand, using the National Association of Realtors' housing affordability index methodology adapted for the Puget Sound region. Vacancy rate projections incorporate both new supply additions and demand-side changes from population growth, income effects, and cross-border migration patterns.

Cost of Living Model

The cost of living index captures direct tax burden changes on households as well as indirect effects through price transmission. BC COL incorporates MSP premiums, corporate tax pass-through to consumer prices (estimated at 10-15% incidence), carbon tax pass-through to fuel and heating costs, and housing cost changes from supply-side interventions. WA COL incorporates sales tax burden on household consumption baskets (weighted by BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey data for the Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue MSA), B&O tax incidence on consumer prices, minimum wage effects on service sector pricing, and capital gains tax effects on investment returns. The competitiveness ranking aggregates these factors with GDP growth, jobs, environmental performance, and cross-border trade efficiency to produce a composite score benchmarked against 50 North American metro regions.

Limitations

This engine uses simplified linear models for illustration. Real-world policy impacts involve complex nonlinear dynamics, time lags, behavioral responses, and federal policy interactions. Projections are directional estimates and should not be used as precise forecasts for fiscal planning or investment decisions. The model does not account for federal tax policy changes, interest rate movements, commodity price fluctuations, or external macroeconomic shocks. Interaction effects between simultaneous policy changes are approximated additively rather than multiplicatively. All baseline figures are derived from 2024 Statistics Canada, US Bureau of Economic Analysis, WA Department of Revenue, BC Ministry of Finance, and CMHC public data. The model assumes ceteris paribus conditions and does not incorporate game-theoretic responses from other jurisdictions.

Reference Information

Data sources, keyboard shortcuts, and usage notes for the scenario engine

Keyboard Shortcuts

Press 1 for Eliminate MSP, 2 for Carbon Tax Double, 3 for WA B&O Reform, 4 for Cascadia Free Trade Zone, R to reset all sliders. Press S to save current scenario, A/B/C to load saved slots. These shortcuts enable rapid scenario comparison during policy workshops and presentations.

Scenario Save System

Use the three save slots (A, B, C) to compare different scenarios. Click a slot to load it, or use the Save Current button to store the active configuration. Saved scenarios persist within the current browser session. This feature enables side-by-side policy comparison during stakeholder consultations and legislative committee reviews.

Primary Data Sources

BC data: Statistics Canada (CANSIM), BC Ministry of Finance Quarterly Report, BC Real Estate Association, Health Canada. WA data: US Bureau of Economic Analysis, WA Department of Revenue, WA Employment Security Department, US Census Bureau ACS. Cross-border: Canada Border Services Agency, US Customs and Border Protection, PNWER annual reports.

Corridor Context

The Cascadia Innovation Corridor encompasses Metro Vancouver (population 2.8M), the Puget Sound region including Seattle, Bellevue, and Tacoma (population 4.0M), and Portland Metro (population 2.5M). The combined economy generates approximately $850B in GDP annually, making it one of the largest cross-border economic zones in North America. Key sectors include technology (Microsoft, Amazon, SAP, Hootsuite, Slack/Salesforce), aerospace (Boeing), clean energy, life sciences, and advanced manufacturing. The corridor processes over $30B in bilateral trade annually through 14 major border crossings. The region shares common geographic, cultural, and economic characteristics including proximity to the Pacific Rim, strong environmental values, highly educated workforces, and a growing innovation ecosystem that competes globally for talent and capital.
