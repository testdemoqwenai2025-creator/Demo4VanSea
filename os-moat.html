1 The Paradox of Global Platforms

A reasonable question: why cannot Bloomberg, S&P Global, Thomson Reuters, or a hypothetical "Cascadia desk" inside Yahoo Finance build this? They have more capital, more salespeople, more brand recognition. The answer is the regional moat.

Global platforms optimize for the average of a national or global user base. The marginal investment to support USMCA Schedule IV §6 citation tracking helps only Cascadia users; the marginal investment to support RCW 39.80 Indigenous preference language helps only Washington users; the marginal investment to support WAC 173-442 cap-and-invest compliance cost calculation helps only WA covered-sector users. Each feature serves perhaps 5,000 paying users globally.

A global platform serving 500,000 users cannot allocate product engineering to features that serve 1% of its base, especially when those features require deep regulatory interpretation that does not transfer to any other region. Regional specificity is a wedge-shaped moat: narrow enough that global players will not enter, deep enough that once we hold it, they cannot catch up without the same years of compounding usage history.

2 The Three Pillars

1

USMCA Rules of Origin — Cross-Border Trade

The United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA, in force July 1, 2020) governs preferential tariff treatment for goods traded among the US, Canada, and Mexico. To claim preferential treatment, a good must meet a regional value content (RVC) threshold specified by its HS code, often supplemented by a tariff shift requirement.

For Cascadia: a contractor building a hydrogen electrolyzer stack with cells fabricated in Burnaby BC, balance-of-plant assembled in Everett WA, and catalyst imported from Mexico must calculate the RVC of the finished good. This requires maintaining the USMCA Schedule IV text, CBP and CBSA rulings, the HS taxonomy, and the user's supply-chain graph.

No global platform maintains this for Cascadia at the depth that daily use requires.

2

BC's Indigenous Procurement Mandates

British Columbia's Indigenous procurement framework is more developed than any US state's: BC Bid Indigenous set-asides, the Indigenous Business and Investment Council (IBIC), the New Relationship Trust (NRT), the BC Procurement Reform Initiative, and TRC Call to Action #92.

The OS must detect on every BC Bid tender whether it is an Indigenous set-aside, identify the eligibility criteria, maintain a consented directory of Indigenous-owned businesses (with explicit opt-in per data sovereignty principle), and recommend JV partners when a non-Indigenous user wants to bid on an Indigenous set-aside.

The Muckleshoot outreach letter is the first step in a years-long relationship. The OS will build analogous relationships with the Squamish, Tsleil-Waututh, Musqueam, Tulalip, Stillaguamish, Puyallup, Suquamish, Umatilla, Yakama, Nez Perce, Warm Springs, Grand Ronde, Siletz, and others as capacity allows. A competitor cannot replicate this without the same years of trust-building.

3

Washington's Climate Commitment Act (CCA)

The CCA (RCW 70A.65, in force January 1, 2023) is Washington state's economy-wide cap-and-invest program covering ~75% of state GHG emissions. It imposes compliance obligations on fuel suppliers, natural gas utilities, electricity importers, and large industrial facilities.

For Cascadia procurement officers, the CCA matters in three ways: bid cost calculation (compliance cost flows through to fuel costs), allowable cost documentation (WAC 173-442 lets contractors claim carbon compliance as allowable cost if documented properly), and the auction-revenue circular flow (CCA funds grants that contractors bid on).

The OS's CCA module maintains current allowance price, the list of covered entities (subset of 1,010 EPA TRI facilities in WA), cost-of-compliance per unit of fuel, and the WAC 173-442 citation for cost-allowability documentation.
