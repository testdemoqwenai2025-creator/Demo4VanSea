⚠ Cascadia Wildfire Outlook

Cascadia's wildfire risk has increased significantly over the past decade due to climate change, forest fuel accumulation, and expanding wildland-urban interface. Key trends:

  • WA: 2020 was worst season on record (1,250+ fires, 713k acres burned). 2023–2025 closer to 10-year average.
  • OR: 2020 Labor Day fires burned 1.2M acres — worst in state history. 2024–2025 above average.
  • BC: 2023 was worst season ever (2.84M hectares burned, 6x avg). 2025 also above average. Smoke impacts Seattle/Portland frequently.
  • Cross-border smoke: BC fires cause 60%+ of smoke days in Seattle/Portland. WA/OR fires cause 30%+ of smoke days in Vancouver/Victoria.

Sources: NIFC annual reports, WA DNR, OR ODF, BC Wildfire Service — all public, taxpayer-funded.

🦅 Migration Insights for Cascadia

Cascadia sits on two major migration corridors:

  • Pacific Flyway: Millions of birds migrate along the coast spring (Mar-May) and fall (Sep-Nov). Key stopovers: Skagit Valley, Boundary Bay, Ridgefield NWR.
  • Salmon runs: Chinook, coho, chum, sockeye return to natal streams Aug-Nov. Best viewing: Cedar River (Sept), Issaquah Salmon Hatchery (Oct), Adams River (Sockeye, every 4 years — next peak 2026).
  • Gray whale migration: ~20,000 whales pass Oregon/WA coast spring (northbound Mar-May) and winter (southbound Dec-Feb). Best viewing: Depoe Bay, Cape Flattery.
  • Elk rut: Sept-Oct. Bugling bulls in Olympic National Park, Mt Rainier NP, Oregon Coast Range.
  • Snow geese: 50,000+ winter in Skagit Valley Nov-March. Spectacular congregations at Fir Island.

Data: iNaturalist seasonal observations + USFWS migration reports + WA Dept of Fish & Wildlife.

▸ DATA SOVEREIGNTY · MOAT #2

Indigenous nations control their own data. Always.

Every environmental data point that touches traditional territory, treaty land, or ceded territory carries a sovereignty layer. The Indigenous nation decides: what gets collected, how it gets presented, who sees it, and how long it persists. FPIC (Free, Prior, and Informed Consent) is the floor, not the ceiling. Every claim is anchored to source via SHA-256, and the monthly Merkle root is published to Polygon — so the audit trail is tamper-evident, irreversible, and survives any future change of administration.

🏛️
Principle 1
Nation Controls Data
The Nation, not the platform, decides what environmental data is collected on their territory, how it’s framed, who sees it. We provide infrastructure; they provide governance.
📜
Principle 2
FPIC Required
Free, Prior, and Informed Consent per UNDRIP Article 32. Consent must precede collection, must be specific, must be revocable. None of this "opt-out later" pattern.
🔗
Principle 3
Provenance Anchored
Every claim carries a SHA-256 hash to its source. Monthly Merkle root published to Polygon blockchain — tamper-evident, irreversible, survives any change of government.
🌍
Principle 4
Two-Eyed Seeing
Western science + Indigenous knowledge sit side-by-side as equal epistemologies (etwa’). Neither subsumes the other. The platform presents both with equal weight in the audit trail.
Why this matters for environmental reviewers: When a project crosses ceded territory, you need an audit trail that survives FOIA delays, agency reorgs, and political turnover. The Polygon blockchain doesn’t care who’s in office. A SHA-256 anchor on the source dataset at time-of-collection is mathematically tamper-evident forever. This is the floor for environmental justice work — not the ceiling.

▸ INDIGENOUS + SOVEREIGNTY GLOSSARY · REFERENCE

Key terms for sovereign data work

Click any term to expand. Bookmark this page — every term a Cascadia environmental reviewer or Indigenous nation staff member needs to know for sovereign data consultations. We cite each back to the original source.

UNDRIP Article 32 · E.O. 14112

Indigenous consent framework: Free (no coercion), Prior (before action), Informed (full information about scope + impacts), Consent (right to give or withhold). Must be specific to the project, not blanket. Must be revocable. The floor for Indigenous engagement, not the ceiling.

When you see it: Any project on or affecting ceded/traditional territory requires FPIC.
UN GA Res. 61/295 (2007)

The international human rights instrument outlining Indigenous rights. Article 32 (FPIC), Article 19 (consultation before laws affecting them). Adopted by Canada in 2010; US endorsed in 2010. BC passed DRIPA in 2019 — first Canadian province to do so.

When you see it: Cited as the legal basis for FPIC requirements in any Cascadia procurement or environmental review.
Mi'kmaq concept · popularized by Albert Marshall

An Indigenous epistemology that sees the strengths of Western science with one eye and Indigenous knowledge with the other — together for the benefit of all. Neither subsumes the other. Cascadia OS presents both with equal weight in the audit trail.

When you see it: Embedded in our Principle 4 of data sovereignty — both epistemologies sit side-by-side in the provenance chain.
BC Bill 41 (2019)

BC provincial legislation implementing UNDRIP. First Canadian province to do so. Mandates alignment of BC laws with UNDRIP. Free, prior, and informed consent is the standard for decisions affecting Indigenous rights.

When you see it: Any BC-based environmental project affecting Indigenous territory triggers DRIPA consultation.
Treaty law · varies by Nation + jurisdiction

Treaty territory: lands formally ceded via signed treaty (e.g., Treaty 8 in BC Peace region). Ceded territory: lands transferred via executive order or allotment without formal treaty (e.g., many WA reservations). Traditional territory: lands a Nation traditionally used + occupied, regardless of formal legal status. All three can trigger consultation requirements.

When you see it: Sovereignty Assessment tool determines which Nations overlap a project's geography + applies the correct consultation standard.
FIPS 180-4 (NIST standard)

The cryptographic hash function used to anchor every claim to its source document. A SHA-256 hash is a 256-bit fingerprint of a file — any change to the source produces a different hash. Cascadia OS publishes a monthly Merkle root (aggregation of all month's hashes) to Polygon blockchain — making the entire audit trail tamper-evident forever.

When you see it: Every compliance matrix row carries a SHA-256 hash. Every provenance chain node anchors to one.
Ralph Merkle (1979) · standard blockchain data structure

A cryptographic aggregation of multiple hashes into a single hash. Cascadia OS publishes one Merkle root per month to Polygon — the root commits to all source hashes from that month without revealing individual claims. Lets you prove a specific claim was anchored without exposing all claims.

When you see it: Monthly anchor in the provenance chain. Survives any future change of administration.
Polygon Proof-of-Stake mainnet · ~$0.01 per transaction

The Ethereum layer-2 blockchain Cascadia OS uses for provenance anchoring. Chosen for: low transaction cost (cents vs dollars on Ethereum mainnet), environmental footprint (Proof-of-Stake = ~99.95% less energy than Proof-of-Work), enterprise adoption, audit trail immutability.

When you see it: Monthly Merkle root anchored here. The blockchain doesn't care who's in office — the anchor is mathematically irreversible.
E.O. 14112 · RCW 43.376 (WA) · DRIPA §3 (BC)

The formal consultation process between a federal/state/provincial government and a federally-recognized Indigenous Nation. Not a stakeholder engagement — it's a sovereign-to-sovereign dialogue. Required before any decision affecting Indigenous rights or territory. Cascadia OS provides the audit trail proving G2G consultation occurred.

When you see it: Any project affecting ceded/traditional territory triggers G2G consultation requirement.
UNDRIP Article 31 · FWS TEK guidance (2023)

Knowledge systems developed by Indigenous peoples over generations of interaction with their traditional territories. Includes environmental observations, species behavior, ecological relationships, seasonal patterns. Protected under UNDRIP Article 31 (Indigenous cultural + intellectual property). Cascadia OS treats IK as equal epistemology to Western science per Two-Eyed Seeing principle.

When you see it: Environmental reviews can incorporate IK alongside Western science — both anchored to source via SHA-256.
▸ CASCADIA SOVEREIGNTY + ENVIRONMENTAL REGULATORY DOCUMENTS

10 official documents for sovereign data work

Every regulation, treaty, executive order, and statute a Cascadia environmental reviewer or Indigenous nation staff member needs to verify. Click any entry to expand + jump straight to the official source.

UN GA Resolution 61/295 (Sept 13, 2007)

The foundational international human rights instrument for Indigenous rights. 46 articles covering self-determination, lands + territories, FPIC, cultural + intellectual property. Adopted by 143 nations (4 opposed: US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand — all later endorsed). The legal basis for FPIC in any Cascadia procurement.

Use it for: Verifying FPIC requirements for any project on ceded/traditional territory.
View UNDRIP full text →
E.O. 14112 · signed Nov 30, 2023 (Biden administration)

Strengthens federal Indigenous consultation requirements + Indigenous-preference provisions. Directs all federal agencies to engage in regular, meaningful, informed consultation with federally-recognized tribes. Effective immediately on signing. Cascadia OS assessor flags E.O. 14112 for any federally-funded Cascadia tender affecting Indigenous territory.

Use it for: Verifying federal consultation requirements on US-side projects.
View E.O. 14112 in Federal Register →
RCW 39.80 · WA State Legislature

Requires Indigenous participation in WA public works projects above $1M. "REQUIRED" set-aside means bid MUST include enrolled tribal member or tribally-owned business as prime or sub. Distinct from E.O. 14112 federal consultation — RCW 39.80 is a state-level procurement equity law.

Use it for: WA public works procurement. Cascadia OS distinguishes "REQUIRED" vs "PREFERRED" in the compliance matrix.
View RCW 39.80 →
BC Bill 41 (Nov 28, 2019)

BC provincial legislation implementing UNDRIP. First Canadian province to do so. Mandates alignment of BC laws with UNDRIP. Requires consent (not just consultation) for decisions affecting Indigenous rights. The strongest Indigenous-rights legislation in North America.

Use it for: BC-based environmental projects. Consent standard is higher than federal consultation standard.
View DRIPA →
42 U.S.C. § 4321 et seq. (1969, amended 2023)

The US federal environmental review law. Requires Environmental Assessments (EA) or Environmental Impact Statements (EIS) for major federal actions significantly affecting the environment. The vehicle for incorporating Indigenous Knowledge + consulting with Nations.

Use it for: Federal-funded Cascadia projects — EIS triggers Indigenous consultation + IK inclusion.

RCW 43.21C (1971)

WA's state-level environmental review law — modeled on NEPA but applies to state/local government actions. Triggers Environmental Impact Statements for major WA projects. Often runs parallel to NEPA for state-funded portions.

Use it for: WA-state-funded Cascadia projects. Cascadia OS assessor flags SEPA compliance.

SBC 2018, c. 51 (BC)

BC's environmental assessment law for major projects. Replaces the 2002 act. Mandates Indigenous participation in environmental assessments — Nations can consent or refuse. Includes a sustainability test + consideration of Indigenous Knowledge.

Use it for: BC major projects (energy, mining, infrastructure) requiring environmental assessment.
View BCEAA →
RCW 43.376 (WA State Legislature)

WA's government-to-government consultation law with federally-recognized Indian tribes. Mandates state agencies to consult with tribes on policies affecting them. Provides the framework for collaboration in environmental reviews + project approvals.

Use it for: WA state-level project consultation requirements with tribes.
View RCW 43.376 →
UNDRIP Article 32 (specific to development projects)

The specific UNDRIP article establishing FPIC for development projects affecting Indigenous territories. Three sub-paragraph: (1) right to free + informed consent prior to approval of any project affecting lands/territories/resources; (2) Nations shall consult + cooperate in good faith; (3) States shall provide effective mechanisms for prevention of any storage/disposal of hazardous materials on Indigenous lands.

Use it for: Specific FPIC invocation in any environmental review affecting Indigenous territory.
View UNDRIP Article 32 →
40 CFR § 1503.3 (EPA NEPA) · 33 CFR § 332 (ACOE) · 25 CFR (BIA)

Three federal agency consultation frameworks: EPA's NEPA implementation (40 CFR § 1503.3 includes tribal consultation); Army Corps of Engineers' Section 404 wetlands permits (33 CFR § 332 includes tribal coordination); Bureau of Indian Affairs (25 CFR) administers federal Indian programs. Each has its own consultation protocol — Cascadia OS assessor applies the correct one per project.

Use it for: Federal environmental reviews affecting Indigenous territory.
